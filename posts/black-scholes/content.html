"Can you write down the Black-Scholes formula?"

If you've ever interviewed for a Quant role, you've probably heard this. I have. I think this is a slightly unfair question. Black-Scholes isn't a formula you can easily carry around in your head. It has two variables and four model parameters, each appearing multiple times, tangled up with an exponential, a logarithm, a square root, and two normal CDFs. Reciting it on demand, under interview pressure, is more a test of nerves than of understanding.

The derivation, however, is a different story. If you know the basics of stochastic calculus, three simple steps are all it takes. Once you derive it, you never need to memorise it again. I think every Quant should do this at least once, pen on paper, as a kind of rite of passage.

This post walks through that derivation step-by-step.

The Setup

The Black-Scholes formula gives us the fair price of a European call option — the right to buy a stock at price \(K\) on a future date \(T\). To derive it, we make the following assumptions.

First, the stock price \(S_t\) follows a geometric Brownian motion. It has a constant drift and is subject to continuous random fluctuations. Importantly, it can never go negative — a basic sanity check for any stock price model.

$$dS_t = \mu S_t \, dt + \sigma S_t \, dW_t$$

where \(\mu\) is the drift, \(\sigma\) (\(> 0\)) the volatility, and \(W_t\) a standard Brownian motion.

Second, the market is arbitrage-free: no trading strategy can generate a guaranteed profit with zero net investment. In liquid markets, any such opportunity would be spotted and eliminated almost immediately, making this a reasonable assumption.

Third, continuous trading: positions can be adjusted at arbitrarily small time intervals, with no delay and no transaction costs. In practice, trading is discrete and costs are real, but this is a useful idealisation that makes the mathematics tractable.

Finally, we assume the volatility (\(\sigma\)) is constant over time. Unlike \(\mu\), \(r\), \(K\), and \(T\), which are either observed or specified, \(\sigma\) is not directly observable in the market and must be estimated. In reality, volatility varies over time and across strike prices — a phenomenon well-documented in options markets. A large body of literature exists on stochastic and local volatility models that address this, but that discussion is out of scope here.

We also assume here that the stock pays no dividends and that the risk-free rate \(r\) remains constant over the life of the option. Versions of the Black-Scholes formula can be derived where these are relaxed.

The Trick

The SDE we wrote down earlier describes the stock price under the real-world measure \(\mathbb{P}\), from which we can derive the distribution of the stock price at any future point in time. The drift \(\mu\) includes a risk premium — the extra return investors demand for bearing the risk of holding the stock. The challenge with pricing an option is that we don't know the right drift to use. Moreover, it has a nonlinear payoff w.r.t the stock price, making its risk profile fundamentally different.

The genius of the original Black-Scholes paper is in sidestepping this problem entirely: by constructing a portfolio of the option and the stock that cancels out all randomness (hedging!), the risk premium becomes irrelevant. What remains is a riskless portfolio that must earn the risk-free rate \(r\) — and that is enough to pin down the option price. This is why \(\mu\) does not appear anywhere in the final formula.

We take a different but equivalent route, one that is arguably more elegant. The risk-neutral measure \(\mathbb{Q}\) is an imaginary probability distribution over future stock prices, constructed so that the price of any traded asset equals the expected value of its future payoffs discounted at the risk-free rate \(r\). It is not a description of how the world actually behaves — but it produces the correct no-arbitrage price. The First Fundamental Theorem of Asset Pricing guarantees that such a measure exists as long as the market is arbitrage-free. The hedging argument above provides the intuition for why: once the risk premium is hedged away, \(r\) is the only discount rate that matters. A more detailed discussion can be found in this post.

The Derivation

Now that we have the necessary background, here's the outline for how we're going to derive our option pricing formula:

  • Step 1: Change the measure from \(\mathbb{P}\) to \(\mathbb{Q}\) — We use Girsanov's theorem to rewrite the stock price SDE under the risk-neutral measure.
  • Step 2: Find the distribution of \(S_T\) under \(\mathbb{Q}\) — We apply Itô's lemma to derive a closed-form expression for the stock price at maturity.
  • Step 3: Compute the option price — We take the expectation under \(\mathbb{Q}\) of the future payoff discounted at a constant risk-free rate \(r\), which reduces to a Gaussian integral.

Step 1: Change of measure from \(\mathbb{P}\) to \(\mathbb{Q}\)

Girsanov's theorem tells us that we can switch from \(\mathbb{P}\) to \(\mathbb{Q}\) by absorbing the market price of risk \(\theta = (\mu - r)/\sigma\) into the Brownian motion. Specifically, under \(\mathbb{Q}\), the following is a standard Brownian motion:

$$\widetilde{W}_t = W_t + \theta t$$

Substituting back, the stock price SDE becomes:

$$dS_t = r S_t \, dt + \sigma S_t \, dW_t$$

The drift \(\mu\) has been replaced by \(r\). We are now working under \(\mathbb{Q}\).

Step 2: Find the distribution of \(S_T\) under \(\mathbb{Q}\)

We apply Itô's lemma to \(f(S_t) = \ln S_t\). Unlike the ordinary chain rule, Itô's lemma picks up an extra term (\(-\frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 \, dt\)) from the quadratic variation of the Brownian motion:

$$d(\ln S_t) = \left(r - \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2\right)dt + \sigma \, d\widetilde{W}_t$$

Integrating from \(0\) to \(T\):

$$\ln S_T = \ln S_0 + \left(r - \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2\right)T + \sigma\widetilde{W}_T$$

Since a standard Brownian motion at time \(T\) is normally distributed with mean zero and variance \(T\), we have:

$$S_T = S_0 \exp\!\left(\left(r - \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2\right)T + \sigma\sqrt{T}\,Z\right)$$

where \(Z \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1)\). Thus, the stock price at maturity is log-normally distributed under \(\mathbb{Q}\).

Step 3: Compute the option price

The call option price is the expectation of the discounted payoff under \(\mathbb{Q}\). With a constant risk-free rate \(r\), we have:

$$C = \mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}\!\left[e^{-rT}(S_T - K)^+\right]$$

The payoff is zero when \(S_T < K\), so the integration only needs to run over the region where \(S_T > K\). Using the log-normal expression for \(S_T\), the condition \(S_T > K\) translates to \(Z > -d_2\), where:

$$d_2 = \frac{\ln(S_0/K) + \left(r - \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2\right)T}{\sigma\sqrt{T}}$$

So the expectation reduces to a Gaussian integral over \(Z > -d_2\):

$$C = e^{-rT} \int_{-d_2}^{\infty} \left(S_0\, e^{(r - \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2)T + \sigma\sqrt{T}\,z} - K\right) \frac{e^{-z^2/2}}{\sqrt{2\pi}} \, dz$$

We split this into two terms, each solvable using standard integration techniques:

$$C = S_0\, N(d_1) - Ke^{-rT} N(d_2)$$ $$d_1 = \frac{\ln(S_0/K) + \left(r + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2\right)T}{\sigma\sqrt{T}}$$ $$d_2 = d_1 - \sigma\sqrt{T}$$

Beyond Black-Scholes

The approach we used here — change to the risk-neutral measure, find the distribution of the underlying, compute the expectation — is not specific to the Black-Scholes setting. It is the standard framework for pricing derivatives across asset classes.

For other European-style payoffs, the same three steps apply with minimal modification. A digital option, for instance, prices immediately as \(e^{-rT}N(d_2)\) (a single line of work!). For path-dependent derivatives like Asian or barrier options, the measure change is identical but the distribution of the relevant quantity is more involved. For derivatives on interest rates, FX, or commodities, the SDE for the underlying changes, and sometimes a different numéraire is more convenient than the risk-free bond.

When no closed form exists — as in most stochastic volatility models — the expectation under \(\mathbb{Q}\) is computed numerically, via Monte Carlo simulation or Fourier methods. But the pricing principle remains the same.
